**Revised:** 2026-11-07.

## Goal

The fiction is a **frame**, and its one job is to **justify the loop**: it explains why the only
lever is *cost*. Keep it as backdrop and subtext — never as exposition. Money is **flavour only**
([core loop](../core-loop.md) §3).

Its voice is **Jim's** — the CEO, who hired you to work the Wrap. Everything here is what Jim knows and
would say, not narration.

## The setting (2048)

- A **stationary-state society**: no births, no new markets, no growth. Population flat or declining.
- **The decline is demographic collapse, not policy.** Nobody chose to stop growing — it happened. The
  fixed pie is a symptom, not a decision.
- The economy is a **fixed pie**. Companies do not grow; they survive by being cheaper.
- **Fuel is expensive.** Transport is a pure cost centre.
- Trade still exists — goods still move between settlements. Only the *market* is fixed. Nobody
  needs more customers; everybody needs lower cost.
- **Nobody has an office.** Premises are a cost the fixed pie cannot carry, so work is remote by
  default — a company is a dispatch terminal in someone's kitchen. Remote work is not a perk here;
  it is the cheapest way to stay alive.

## Who you are — the new employee

- **You are the new hire.** The firm's second person: remote seat 2, working the Wrap from your own
  kitchen. You never speak and are never named; the job card's manual is addressed to you.
- **You are the first in years.** The manual still opens *"Welcome, new employee"*, written for a hire
  nobody expected. You did arrive — there was just nowhere to arrive at.

## Jim — the CEO

- **The firm is Our Space Corp.** The grand suffix on a two-person company is the joke. The name
  appears where the firm introduces itself: Jim's first message (job 1's receipt) and the header of
  every job card.
- **Jim runs the company.** A small firm; he is the **CEO** and the **dispatcher** — he takes the
  contracts and hands them to you, because you are the only one there is.
- **The Wrap is not his idea.** He is not an enthusiast — he is a man doing his job with a machine he
  does not fully trust, and asking you to trust it for him.
- **The voice is his.** Dry, tired, adult; understatement, not exclamation. He never explains the
  physics — he complains about it.
- **He is the face and voice of the job.** Jim introduces each job (one line on his call panel, across the
  field from the contract card) and relays the receipt or the silence at the end. He is the only voice in the run.
- **Two consoles, two kitchens.** Jim works from his kitchen, you from yours; the company is two people
  who have never shared a room.

## The tech: spacetime wrapping

- A **new system** built by a tech team to manage transport: **wrapping spacetime** — the **Wrap**
  ([glossary](../glossary.md)).
- It is **new and experimental**, which justifies why the player is learning it and why it *is* the
  game.
- **Why it exists.** The world put a **hard cap on fuel**, so the company could not burn more — and
  found a way to spend **entropy** directly instead. It is untested and, in principle, can
  **literally destroy the universe**.
- **It is the company's new plan.** In a fixed pie, the Wrap is how the firm means to make more money —
  and **Jim needs someone to run it** — ready or not, that is you.

## The console — the office is wherever you are

- **The Wrap is portable.** The company issues a **console**: the Wrap's client, a dispatch terminal
  that projects the bubble into whatever room it stands in. A kitchen is a valid dispatch station; the
  universe does not care where the desk is.
- **This is why the bubble is in your room.** The player's real room is not a convenience of the medium —
  in the fiction, the console put the space there. Passthrough is the product working as intended.
- **There is no office.** Our Space Corp is two people in two kitchens, and the tech team is remote too,
  which is why it stays text and never a face. Nobody has met the client. In a fixed pie, a company that
  survives is one with no premises and no commute.
- **The map rides the console.** Between jobs the contract map sits on the console, on your own table.
  You slide the cube onto a contract and pinch into it until its patch of spacetime fills the room. The
  console never leaves the room, because there is nowhere to go.
- **The pitch.** Jim repeats the sales line without enthusiasm: *"Works everywhere."* The bleak reading
  is the true one — everywhere is the same kitchen, at night, alone.
- **No commute, no presence, no second system.** Remote means **sedentary**: the player never walks to
  the console or carries it between rooms. The console is furniture and the map; the cube stays the
  instrument.
- **The new employee is you.** The manual addresses a new employee and expects an office; you arrived
  in a kitchen. That gap is the manual's arc, not a second character.

## The progression (the frame for the run)

The run is the company **proving the Wrap**, one delivery at a time. All backdrop:

- **The map is the contracts.** Each **numbered node** is a prepared delivery; the number is the tech
  team's order of readiness, not a menu. Pinching into a contract brings its patch of spacetime into
  the room ([progression](../systems/progression.md)).
- **The order is calibration.** The Wrap is new, so it is proven **step by step** — each delivery that
  lands widens what the company will wrap next (more bodies, tighter promises). What the player feels as
  difficulty is the tech team's growing nerve. Nothing else gates the run: no credits, no rank.
- **The stars are the margin.** The eco rating is the **entropy** the delivery did *not* spend. A firm
  that survives by being cheaper lives on that number; a three-star job is one the ledger is proud of.
- **The map fills with the network.** A solved cube **lights**, and the lit map *is* the company's
  reach — a small firm quietly covering more of the sector. That filling is the progression feel.

The **arrival** is the customer's promise — the one thing the company must not break. Editing little
and arriving are the same play; the stars are where it shows up as a number.

**A contract can be a multi-ship run.** Some promises need more than one ship; the company flies
them together, and the promise is the same — everything arrives.

## Episodes

The story is told in **episodes**, one per chapter (decided 2026-11-11). Each episode says **one thing
about the world** through Jim's lines and the manual, the way each chapter teaches one idea through the
puzzles. The clients stay absurd; the episode's point lives in what Jim books and how he sounds about
it. An episode never explains itself and never resolves the bleak reading.

| Episode | Chapter | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — the Wrap | the seven-job first episode | The firm bets on a machine nobody trusts, and the new employee makes it work. |
| 2 — the fleet | the multi-ship jobs | It works, so the firm gets greedy: more ships on the same entropy, sold as green. Greed is eco-friendly, and Jim is not sure that is good news. |

**The chapter opener carries the episode; the job intro carries the lesson.** Jim opens a chapter
once, before its first job and with no field in the room: the world beat (what the firm is doing now,
and why). Then the job starts as usual with its own intro line, which stays the teaching line. A replay
never repeats the opener. Chapter 1 has none: job 1's receipt is Jim's introduction.

The game is never finished in the fiction: the last episode closes with Jim out of contracts and the
tech team promising more. The next episode is the
next chapter; seeds for it live in §Seeds (externalised costs, *tomorrow the time*).

## The customers

- The other end of every contract is a **client** — a settlement or firm in the same stationary economy.
- **Names are absurd on purpose** (random, comic): the sector is bleak, the paperwork is not. It keeps
  the tone from tipping into misery.
- The client is **never a face** — a name, a contract, a promise.

## The tech team

- **Text, not a voice.** The team that built the Wrap is heard only through its **onboarding manual** —
  the entry on each job card, short, dry, slightly out of date. Jim reads the room; the manual reads the
  rules. Not a cast and not a second voice; a colleague in the fiction, never on screen.
- **The console is their look.** Everything the tech team "says" on screen — the manual entry on the
  job card and the gesture prompts — reads as the Wrap's dispatch terminal: a thin frame, monospace
  type, phosphor line art in the UI accent, a faint scanline/flicker, a blinking cursor; it boots in on
  show and cuts off like a CRT on hide. Jim is not the console's
  voice — he is the person, with his face — but he reaches you **through** it: his panel is a call on
  the console, in the console's frame, across the field from the contract card, with his own face and words
  rather than terminal type.
- **No reactive hints during the solve.** The job card is the contract, not a hint: the selection map
  shows the full contract, and a smaller copy stays available during the run as the job's reference.
  Otherwise help comes at the start (Jim's intro line, a gesture prompt the first time a gesture is
  needed) and at the end (the complete or fail message); nothing reacts to the player being stuck or
  offers a mid-solve tip.
- **Their roadmap slogan:** *"Today the space, tomorrow the time."* The Wrap wraps **space** today;
  wrapping **time** is the team's next promise — a future feature (or DLC), not this run.

## "Revenue is relative"

The tagline. Three readings stack:

1. **Fixed pie** — revenue is a *share*, not a total. You keep more of it by spending less.
2. **Frame-dependent** — value is measured against a clock, and clocks are frame-dependent. The
   same delivery is worth different amounts depending on whose time you settle it in.
3. **The physics name.**

The payoff is (3): the fiction's economics and the mechanic's physics are the **same idea**.

**How to use it:** as background for now (decided 2026-11-10; it was cut from the boot screen). If an obvious
moment appears in the game's lines, it may surface once as an *in-world* slogan — a poster the tech
team hung, which Jim rolls his eyes at.
**Not** as the pitch line: the double meaning only lands once the player knows the game is about
relativity.

## Greed is eco-friendly

The theme. Normally eco and profit pull apart and you build a choice around the gap. Here the
physics **collapses** them: a gravity assist is *literally* getting something for nothing by
reading the field better. The greedy play **is** the green play. No scold, no guilt.

- **No dilemma is the feature** — but know where the fault line is. It already exists:
  **entropy-greed vs the promise.** Editing little is cheap, and the victim is the customer's delivery.
- **Darker, later (optional):** the cheap path grazes someone's home, or parks the crew in a time
  well — a social or human cost, externalised off the ledger.

## The bleak reading (keep it ambiguous)

Society only gets greener because it **stopped growing and got desperate** — scarcity, not virtue.
Let the player read it as triumph *or* satire. Do not resolve it in dialogue; the ambiguity is what
makes a small game stick.

## In the game — show, don't tell

This doc explains the world; the game must never say it. Each idea above reaches the player through
an object, not a line. Where each one lives:

| Idea | What the player sees | State |
|---|---|---|
| It is a company console | The console boots at app start: *OUR SPACE CORP · DISPATCH CONSOLE · REMOTE SEAT 2* | planned (the first 30 seconds) |
| You are on your own, at your hours | The job card header carries the console's clock, your real local time | live |
| The manual is written to you, and keeps being rewritten | The manual line reads *MANUAL r2, r3…*: a new revision per delivery | live |
| Jim is remote too | His call panel: his kitchen behind him (night window, mug), a faint call timestamp | planned (art) |
| The lit map is the firm's reach | A coverage count on the console (*SECTOR COVERAGE 7/10*) | idea ([explorable map](../../ideas/explorable-map.md)) |
| "Revenue is relative" | Nothing yet: background for writing the world. Add it only where a game line earns it (after the player has seen spending less pay more); never at boot, never spoken | background |

## Guardrails

- **Backdrop, not exposition.** Nobody says "revenue is relative" out loud. An entropy-saved number
  climbing while margin climbs *is* the statement.
- **One term per idea** — the fixed terms are in the [glossary](../glossary.md). No other physics
  jargon.
- **Tone: melancholy and adult** — a company that survives, not one that conquers.
- **Don't stack puns in the pitch.** One signature pun ("Our Space") plus one in-world slogan.

## Seeds (later)

- **Externalised costs.** A cheap route that grazes an inhabited world, or a crew put in a time
  well, as a social/human price hidden from the ledger.
- **The manual's arc.** The entries open cheerful and end bleak — they are written to "the new employee",
  and that is you. The arc lives in the text and its revisions, not a second character.
- **Per-job launch (the narrative fuel).** Once the launch is per-job, each job's *given* launch
  becomes a story beat — a launch that would fail, made to land.
- **The lit map at home.** The map as a physical object the firm lives around — lit cubes as quiet
  pride, dark ones as the work left.

## Canon (settled)

- **Static because demographic collapse** — nobody chose it; the fixed pie is a symptom.
- **Jim, the CEO**, is the voice; **the player is the new employee** on the second console, working the
  Wrap from their own kitchen.
- **The firm is Our Space Corp.** — named in Jim's introduction and on the job cards.
- **The Wrap is the company's new plan** to make more money; Jim bets the firm on it and you run it.
- **Clients have absurd names**; the client is never a face.
- **Jim is the only voice** — the face of the job intro and the end message. The tech team is text (the
  manual on the job card); there are no reactive in-solve hints, and the contract card is a static
  reference.
- **Work is remote and the console is the company's.** The company issues the console; it projects the
  bubble into the player's room and carries the map between jobs. MR anchoring is the fiction, not a
  medium convenience.
- **The reading stays ambiguous** — triumph or satire, unresolved.
